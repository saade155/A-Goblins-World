# Mood Brief -- "This Goblin's World"

*Creative direction for the art team. Sketch the feeling, not the pixels.*

---

## The Core Feel

The world starts as a dark, scary place that becomes YOUR adventure. Underground is oppressive and unknown. Every shadow could hide danger or treasure. But as you explore, light torches, push deeper -- the fear transforms into mastery. The unknown becomes YOUR territory.

Four emotional pillars drive everything:

- **Wonder** -- moments that make you stop and stare
- **Fear** -- the darkness has weight, the unknown has teeth
- **Awe** -- scale, beauty, spectacle earned through danger
- **Curiosity** -- that thing in the distance pulling you forward

---

## Art Direction: Dark While Colorful

This is the visual identity of the game in five words: **dark while also being colorful.**

Not dark as in desaturated and grim. Dark as in the shadows are deep, but what the light touches is vivid. A mushroom in pitch black emits rich teal and purple. Lava in a black cavern is searingly orange. Your torch casts warm gold on stone that's otherwise invisible. The darkness makes the color MORE intense, not less.

Style references: Ori and the Blind Forest, Dead Cells. Painterly lighting with bold silhouettes. Soft edges, atmospheric color, no hard outlines. The world is defined by light and contrast, not ink lines. Characters and environment read through silhouette and color, not line art.

NOT pixel art. NOT cel-shaded. NOT realistic. Whimsical and fantastical but taken completely seriously. The world is strange and beautiful and dangerous.

### The Contrast Rule

- Shadows are not grey. They are deep blue, deep purple, near-black with hue. Shadows have color.
- Lit areas are not just bright -- they are saturated. Rich. Color is turned up where light exists.
- The negative space (darkness) is as important as what you can see. Darkness is not empty -- it is heavy, present, pressing in.

### Light Defines Identity

Every biome's identity IS its light. Change the light, change the world. The same stone wall looks completely different under torchlight versus bioluminescence versus lava glow.

- **Torchlight**: Warm amber-gold. Small radius. Your only friend underground. Makes stone warm and alive in a tiny circle, everything beyond is deep shadow.
- **Bioluminescence**: Cool teal, purple, soft pink. Ambient. The environment itself glows. Your torch becomes secondary. Dreamlike.
- **Lava light**: Hot orange-red. Aggressive. Washes everything warm. No cool shadows exist here -- everything is angry and lit.
- **Crystal refraction**: Prismatic. Shifting colors -- pink, blue, green. Unpredictable. Light bounces and scatters in ways that make distance hard to judge.
- **Sunlight**: Warm gold-green on the surface. Dappled through canopy. The only natural, safe-feeling light in the game.
- **Absence of light**: The Abyss. Your torch is dimmer here. Light doesn't travel as far. The darkness actively resists illumination.

Color palette is not a swatch to pick from -- it emerges from the interaction of materials and light. The same dirt is golden-brown in torchlight, sickly green under bioluminescence, deep red near lava. One material, many moods.

## Design Philosophy

### Every Choice Is Correct

The game never punishes curiosity. Go up, go down, explore sideways -- every direction is rewarded differently. There are no wrong doors. There are no quest markers, no glowing waypoints, no "go here next" prompts. The world shows you things in the distance and lets you decide.

### No Instructions. Intuitive Design.

Old games dropped you in a hostile world with no tutorials. This game does the same -- but not through laziness. Through intentional design. The world communicates through visual language, not text boxes. A wall looks breakable. Ore looks valuable. A wolf looks dangerous. The dead miner's pickaxe is in his hand near the wall he almost broke through. You understand immediately. No popup needed.

No instructions does not mean no clarity. It means the world itself teaches through consequence and intuition. Intuitive design is harder than tutorials, but it respects the player.

### Nowhere Is Safe. Safety Is Earned.

The surface is not a peaceful starting zone. Wolves, boars, bears roam the forest. Caves are full of spiders, insects, and worse. NOWHERE is safe by default -- you MAKE it safe. You clear the wolves from around your home. You light torches in the caves near your base. You push the danger back one step at a time.

Your territory grows as you do. Safety is not given -- it is carved out of a hostile world, one fight at a time.

### Rise. Protect. Endure.

The game does not hold your hand because the world does not care about you. You are a goblin in rags with a dead miner's pickaxe. Everything from here is earned. Skills level because you survived enough to get better. Equipment matters because without it you die. Base building is not cosmetic -- it is survival.

## The Opening

### Cutscene

A slideshow/cutscene: a goblin discovers a mysterious doorway. Curiosity wins. They step through. They stumble, fall, lose consciousness. Darkness.

### Waking Up

The player comes to. A narrow passage, barely any light. One way to go. No tools, no skills, wearing rags -- normal for a goblin. Nothing special about you.

They find a body. A dead miner -- or adventurer, someone unknown. They look to have been trying to escape. They had a pickaxe. They were almost through the wall in front of them. They failed.

You take the pickaxe. Your first tool, inherited from someone who died trying to do what you are about to do. The game has told you everything you need to know without a single word of instruction: this place kills people.

### The First Choice

Your first instinct is to continue where they left off. You swing the pickaxe at the wall they almost broke through. You succeed -- they were one hit away. That hits hard. They almost made it.

On the other side: a cave system. Light filtering down from somewhere above. Darkness extending below. Up is survival. Down is curiosity.

The game's entire identity in one moment. Every choice is correct.

### The Surface

Most players go up. They should -- they need light, resources, safety. They climb, they dig, they find the surface. A forest biome. Water nearby. Open sky. After the claustrophobia of the intro cave, it feels freeing. Relieving.

Then the first wolf appears. The surface is not safe. Underground, danger was contained -- one tunnel, one direction, threats in front of you. Up here, danger is everywhere. Open space means exposed. The forest gave you a false sense of relief.

Some players will want to go back underground where it is predictable.

### The Loop

The early game emerges naturally:
- Surface: building, sustaining, claiming territory from wildlife
- Underground: risk, reward, progression -- ores, materials, discovery
- The pull: every trip underground, background layers show things you have not reached yet. You go a little further each time.

The dead miner at the start is not just backstory. It is a warning about what happens to everyone who comes here. You are the exception because you refuse to stop.

### Intro Cave Generation

The intro cave is procedurally generated -- different shape, length, and layout every world. But the story beats are always the same:
1. Wake up, one way to go
2. Find the body, get the pickaxe
3. Break through where they failed
4. Cave opens -- light above, dark below, choose

The structure is procedural. The story is fixed. Every world feels fresh but every player shares the same origin moment.

## The Depth Philosophy

This is the most important concept for the art team to internalize:

**Every background layer is a real place you can reach.**

In most 2D games, parallax backgrounds are painted scenery -- decoration. In This Goblin's World, what you see in the background is actual playable space. That glowing mushroom cluster behind the cave wall? You'll stand next to it when you find the way in. That ruined tower silhouetted two layers deep? There's a dungeon entrance at its base.

The world has depth layers -- foreground (where you are), and multiple background planes visible through gaps, cracks, and openings in the terrain. These background layers are dimmer, slightly smaller (parallax perspective), and tinted by atmosphere and distance. But they're REAL. The game never lies about what's back there.

This means every piece of background art isn't a backdrop -- it's a preview of where you're going next.

### How Depth Creates Discovery

- You're mining through dark stone. Through a crack in the wall, you see a faint green glow two layers back. Bioluminescent mushrooms. You can't reach them yet. But now you NEED to find the way in.
- Approaching a dungeon, the background starts showing carved stone walls, pillars, arched doorways -- ruins getting denser as you get closer. You know something massive is nearby before you find the entrance.
- A volcanic glow visible across three depth layers, growing brighter and larger as you approach the Volcanic Depths. The heat becomes visual before it becomes mechanical.
- Surface: standing on a hill, you can see a frozen mountain range in the far background layer, a desert stretching in another direction. The world shows you where adventure lives.

### Transition Points

Where depth layers connect, there should be visual language that says "you can go deeper here":

- A crack wide enough to squeeze through
- A crumbling wall with light bleeding from behind
- A ledge overlooking a vast cavern below or behind
- A doorway carved into rock (dungeon entrance)
- A shimmering portal frame (realm transition)

## Emotional Palette Per Biome

Each biome isn't just a tileset -- it's a mood shift. The contrast between biomes is what creates the emotional journey.

---

### Surface Biomes

#### Forest -- Safety, Home

The exhale. Where you return to after the underground.

Warm greens, dappled light, gentle movement -- leaves, grass swaying. Should feel like the one place in the world that's genuinely safe. Not boring. Alive and welcoming. A campfire at the end of a long expedition.

**Palette:** warm greens, golden light, earth browns.

#### Desert -- Harsh Openness

Exposed. Nowhere to hide. The sun is not your friend.

Vast, flat, punishing brightness after the darkness underground. Sandy yellows bleached by heat. Mirages. Cracked earth. Ancient things buried under sand -- hints of structures below the surface.

**Palette:** bleached yellows, orange heat haze, bone white.

#### Swamp -- Unease

Something is wrong here. The water is wrong. The trees are wrong.

Murky greens and browns. Fog sitting heavy on the water. Things moving just under the surface. Bubbles rising. Not outright terrifying -- just deeply uncomfortable. You don't want to linger.

**Palette:** sickly greens, muddy browns, grey fog, poisonous purple accents.

#### Mountains -- Grandeur and Exposure

Verticality. Looking up and feeling small. Looking down and feeling scared.

Grey stone, dramatic cliff faces, wind-swept. Caves visible in cliff walls across the depth layers. The mountain itself is a landmark visible from other biomes in the background.

**Palette:** cool greys, slate blue, stark white highlights.

#### Snowy Peaks -- Isolation

Cold silence. The world is muted. Sound doesn't carry.

Everything is white and pale blue. Minimal contrast. Beautiful in a way that makes you feel alone. Ice formations catching light. Frozen waterfalls.

**Palette:** white, pale blue, silver, cold purple shadows.

#### Beach -- Transition

Brief respite between land and sea. Warm but temporary.

Soft sand, gentle waves, driftwood. The ocean stretching out in the background -- vast and unknowable.

**Palette:** warm sand, turquoise water, white foam.

#### Ocean -- The Unknown Horizontal

What's out there? You can see the water extending into background layers.

Dark water hiding what's below. Surface shimmers, depths don't.

**Palette:** deep blue-green, dark navy depths, scattered light on surface.

---

### Underground Biomes

#### Standard Caverns -- Tension, Learning

This is where most players spend their first hours underground. It should feel dangerous but survivable.

Dark. Your torch is a small island of warm light in cold darkness. Beyond the light, shapes are uncertain. Stone walls are rough, uneven, natural. Nothing man-made. You're the first one here.

The darkness beyond your light should feel like it has DEPTH -- background layers showing faint cave shapes receding into nothing.

**Palette:** dark greys, warm torchlight orange in a small radius, deep shadow blue-black beyond.

#### Sandy Hollows -- Dry, Ancient

The desert extends underground. Sandstone walls, dry air, dust particles visible in your torchlight.

Fossils embedded in walls. Ancient seabeds turned to stone. More open than Standard Caverns -- wider passages, fewer tight squeezes. Feels old. Whatever lived here is long gone.

**Palette:** sandy tan, dusty orange, fossil white, warm amber light.

#### Swamp Depths -- Claustrophobia, Rot

The walls are wet. Everything drips. The air is thick.

Tight passages. Low ceilings. You feel the weight of earth above you. Bioluminescent mold on walls gives off a sickly green ambient glow -- just enough to make the darkness worse, not better. Roots pushing through ceilings. The swamp above is reaching down.

**Palette:** dark wet green, slime yellow-green glow, rotting brown, black water.

#### Fungal Grove -- Wonder, Alien Beauty

THE emotional payoff of early underground exploration. First time the world takes your breath away.

Massive cavern. After tight caves, the space itself is the spectacle. Giant mushrooms towering into background depth layers. Bioluminescent everything -- blue, purple, teal, soft pink. The glow here replaces torchlight. Your torch feels unnecessary. The biome itself is alive with light.

Spores drifting like underwater snow. Soft, dreamlike movement everywhere. Background layers show even BIGGER mushroom structures, forests of fungi receding into glowing depths.

This should feel like stepping into another world -- even though you're still in the main one.

**Palette:** deep purple-blue shadows, teal and cyan bioluminescence, soft pink accents, warm amber spore glow.

#### Frozen Caverns -- Isolation, Echoing Vastness

Cold has a visual weight. Everything is sharp edges and reflections.

Ice formations -- stalactites, frozen waterfalls, crystal-clear ice showing what's frozen inside. The ice has fractured and split, creating maze-like networks. Background layers show vast frozen lakes, ice bridges spanning chasms.

Blue. Everything is blue. But not friendly blue -- cold, hard, unyielding blue.

**Palette:** ice blue, deep navy shadows, white crystalline highlights, pale cyan ambient glow.

#### Volcanic Depths -- Dread, Power

You feel this biome before you see it. The background layers glow red-orange from far away.

Lava provides light -- angry, shifting, red-orange. Your torch is irrelevant. The world is lit by fire. The rock itself looks stressed -- cracked, glowing at the seams, ready to break. Open caverns with lava lakes visible in background layers. Magma falls cascading between depth planes. Heat distortion. The air shimmers. Nothing is quite still.

This is endgame territory and it should FEEL like it. Everything here says "you shouldn't be here yet."

**Palette:** deep black rock, red-orange lava glow, angry amber, dark red shadows, occasional blue-hot spots.

#### Crystal Caverns -- Awe, Reward

You earned this. The deepest standard biome. The payoff for being brave.

Crystals catch and refract any light source -- your torch creates prismatic rainbows across walls. Massive crystal formations growing through multiple depth layers. Not warm, not cold. Alien. The crystals have their own internal light -- soft, pulsing, like a heartbeat.

Geometric beauty in contrast to the organic chaos of every other biome. Background layers show crystal forests, impossible formations, light refracting in ways that make depth hard to judge.

**Palette:** deep amethyst purple, crystal white, prismatic rainbow refractions, soft internal glow -- pink, blue, green shifting.

---

### Sister Realms (Separate Worlds)

#### The Abyss

Everything is wrong. The rules you learned don't apply.

Darkness here is different -- not absence of light but presence of dark. Your torch is dimmer. Light doesn't travel as far. Impossible geometry hints. Passages that feel like they shouldn't connect. Scale that doesn't make sense. The background layers show things that are hard to process -- vast shapes, movement at scale that implies something enormous.

You are not welcome here. The world itself resists you.

**Palette:** void black, deep purple veins, sickly green accents, your torchlight struggles -- warm but small.

#### Mushroom Realm (accessed from Fungal Grove)

The Fungal Grove was a preview. This is the full thing.

An entire world of fungi. The sky is a mushroom canopy. The ground is mycelium. Everything is alive and interconnected. Step on something and things react three layers deep. Beautiful but unsettling -- this ecosystem doesn't need you and doesn't care about you.

**Palette:** the Fungal Grove palette amplified -- more saturated, more varied, more alive.

## Dungeon Visual Language

Dungeons are discovered, not placed on a map. The world hints at them through background layers before you find the entrance.

### Approach (background teasing)

As you get close to a dungeon, the background layers begin showing man-made (or creature-made) elements: carved walls, pillars, doorframes, collapsed architecture.

This should be gradual -- first a single pillar glimpsed through a crack, then more structure visible, then unmistakable ruins filling the background. The dungeon's biome colors its ruins -- jungle temple is overgrown green stone, ice fortress is carved blue ice, volcanic forge has magma channels in its walls.

### Entrance

Always dramatic. A doorway, a gate, a carved opening. Unmistakable -- you KNOW this is a dungeon entrance. Light or energy emanating from inside, visible from a distance. Draws the eye.

Scale shift -- the entrance should feel bigger than the player. You're entering something important.

### Interior

Each dungeon has its own visual identity tied to its biome but distinct from the natural caves. Architecture. Intentional design. Someone or something BUILT this.

Unique lighting -- magical torches, glowing runes, lava channels, crystal formations used as light sources. Depth layers inside dungeons show rooms, corridors, treasures you haven't reached yet.

## Key Art Concepts to Sketch First

For the artists getting started, these are the highest-value concept pieces:

1. **The torch radius** -- A goblin standing in Standard Caverns. Warm torchlight in a small circle. Absolute darkness beyond. Background layer barely visible through a crack -- just enough to see shapes. This is the CORE visual of the game.

2. **Breaking into the Fungal Grove** -- The moment of transition. Tight dark cavern opening into a vast bioluminescent space. The contrast. The scale shift. The wonder.

3. **Background dungeon tease** -- Mining through stone. In the background layer, carved pillars and archways visible. Something is back there. You can't reach it yet.

4. **Volcanic Depths approach** -- Standing at the edge where stone becomes volcanic rock. Background layers glowing red-orange. Lava visible far in the distance through multiple depth planes. Dread.

5. **Depth layer transition** -- The goblin standing at a crack in the wall. On the other side (background layer), a lit chamber with something interesting. The visual language of "you can go there."

6. **Surface to underground** -- Standing at a cave entrance on the forest surface. Warm sunlight behind, darkness below. The threshold between safety and adventure.

## Technical Notes for Artists

- Tile size is 16x16 pixels in the game grid, but concept art doesn't need to be at game resolution. Sketch the MOOD, we'll translate to tiles later.
- The player (goblin) is roughly 3 tiles tall, 2 tiles wide.
- Depth layers appear dimmer and slightly smaller (parallax scaling). Background layer 1 is maybe 70% brightness with slight blue-shift. Layer 2 is 50% brightness, more blue. Layer 3 is barely visible shapes.
- Light sources are critical -- torches, bioluminescence, lava, crystals all provide light with different colors and behaviors.
- Fog of war means undiscovered areas are completely hidden (dark fog). The concept art should show the REVEALED world -- what it looks like once you've been there.

## Team Roles and Workflow

### The Team

Three roles with complementary strengths:

**Creative Director / Engine Developer**
- Defines the vision and creative direction
- Builds the game engine, rendering pipeline, lighting systems
- Makes architectural and design decisions
- Does NOT need to draw or model -- the vision IS the art contribution

**Concept Artist**
- Pen and paper artist
- Concept sketches, mood pieces, creature designs, environment composition
- Defines what things LOOK like -- shapes, silhouettes, proportions, character
- The six priority sketches in this document are the starting point

**3D Artist**
- Blender / 3D modeling experience
- Takes concept art and builds models, textures, animations
- Turns sketches into game-ready assets

### The Pipeline (In Progress)

The art pipeline is still being determined. One approach being explored is 3D-to-sprite rendering (model in Blender, render to sprite sheets via orthographic camera and shaders). The final workflow will be decided as the team experiments with different approaches.

Under that potential pipeline, the flow would look like:

1. Creative Director describes the vision (this document)
2. Concept Artist sketches concepts on paper
3. Creative Director and Concept Artist iterate until the feel is right
4. 3D Artist models and textures based on approved concepts
5. Models are rendered to sprite sheets through an engine pipeline
6. Assets go into the game with biome-appropriate lighting

Nobody does the part they are worst at. The technical brain builds systems. The pen-and-paper artist defines the look. The 3D artist builds the production assets. The exact production steps between "approved concept" and "in-game sprite" will solidify as the team finds what works best.
